What this project shows

Design thinking

Starting from people's real experiences, exploring three directions, and choosing one for clear reasons.
Hands-on expertise

A content system, a haptic language, and a working SwiftUI prototype across iPhone, Live Activity, and Apple Watch.
Problem-solving

Designing around real platform limits and making sure a safety alert can never be missed.

The problem

About 430 million people worldwide have disabling hearing loss (World Health Organization). At live events, much of the experience still arrives as sound:

  • Announcements: delays, gate changes, and safety instructions come over the PA system.
  • Words: lyrics, on-stage banter, and play-by-play are missed.
  • Energy: the build before a drop, or the crowd erupting after a goal. Fans see the reaction but miss the cue.

Venues may offer interpreters or caption screens, but often only on request, in one spot, and fans must look away from the stage to follow them.

How might we help Deaf and hard-of-hearing fans follow every moment without taking their eyes off the stage?

Part 1

Design thinking

Who it's for

PersonSituationMain need
Deaf concertgoerUses ASL; goes to concerts with hearing friendsFollow lyrics and banter; feel the music
Hard-of-hearing sports fanLost hearing later in life; hearing aids struggle with crowd noiseNever miss a big play or an announcement
Hearing companionAttends with a Deaf friend or family memberShare the moment without translating everything

Research

I'm interviewing 6–8 Deaf and hard-of-hearing fans, both ASL users and non-signers, observing live events with participants, and reviewing existing tools such as Live Captions, Music Haptics, and venue captioning.

Hypotheses I'm testing

  • Fans want to keep their eyes on the stage, so the wrist matters more than the phone.
  • Safety announcements are the most important and least accessible content.
  • Fans want to feel the energy, not just read about it.

What already exists

I reviewed six tools fans can use today. Each solves part of the problem, but none brings live venue content, alert priority, and haptics together on devices fans already own.

ToolWhat it does wellGap at live events
Stadium caption boardsNo device needed; captions play-by-play and PA announcements on video boardsOne fixed spot, so fans look away from the action; varies by venue
GalaPro (theater caption app)Captions on your phone, synced to the show's soundboard; other languagesPhone in hand; needs a venue partnership; mainly theater
Music: Not Impossible (haptic vest)Rich feeling: vibrations mapped to instruments across the bodySpecial hardware, only at partner events
Live Captions (Apple)Real-time, on-device captions of speech and audioGeneral purpose; not built around venue content like set lists, speakers, or alert priority
Music Haptics (Apple)iPhone taps and textures synced to a song's rhythmDesigned for recorded music, not a live show and its big moments
Sound Recognition (Apple)Alerts for sounds like sirens and alarmsSays a sound happened, not what an announcement said

The opportunity: combine what each does best: venue-synced content (like GalaPro), a feeling of the music (like the haptic vest and Music Haptics), and clear alerts, on the iPhone and Apple Watch fans already carry.

Three directions I explored

DirectionStrengthWeakness
A. Phone onlySimple; everything in one placeFans look down and miss the stage
B. Phone + Live Activity + WatchDetail when wanted, glances when needed, feeling on the wristMore surfaces to keep consistent
C. Venue screensNo device neededOne spot only, and depends on each venue

I chose B because it lets each surface do one job well: the iPhone for every word, the Live Activity for a quick glance, and Apple Watch for what you feel. It's the only direction that keeps fans' eyes on the stage. The cost is consistency across surfaces, which I solved with one shared content system.

A haptic language you can learn in 30 seconds

Bar height shows strength; wide bars are continuous vibrations. These are the exact patterns in the iPhone prototype (Core Haptics). Apple Watch uses built-in haptic types, so its patterns are built by sequencing them with similar timing.

Prototype

One SwiftUI project with three parts, driven by a simulated 90-second concert that plays captions, alerts, and haptics on a timeline.

  • iPhone: SwiftUI, Core Haptics, VoiceOver announcements for alerts
  • Live Activity: ActivityKit and WidgetKit for the Lock Screen, Dynamic Island, and Apple Watch Smart Stack
  • Apple Watch: SwiftUI and WatchKit haptics, started in sync through WatchConnectivity

Part 3

Problem-solving

Constraints and the decisions they shaped

ProblemOptionsDecision and why
Live Activities are built for short, glanceable updates, not a constant stream of wordsStream captions in the Live Activity; keep them in the appFull captions stay in the app; the Live Activity shows key moments and alerts. Each surface does one job well.
A missed safety alert is the worst possible failureBanner; full screen; repeating hapticFull screen on every device, VoiceOver announcement, and a haptic that repeats until the fan taps "I'm on my way."
Apple Watch offers a fixed set of haptic typesCustom patterns; sequences of built-in typesEach signal is a different sequence and rhythm, so all five still feel distinct.
Haptics should be optional (Apple Human Interface Guidelines), but a missed safety alert is dangerousLet people turn off all haptics; keep some always onA Haptics switch turns off music and moment haptics. Safety alerts still vibrate, and every haptic also has a visual label.
Color alone fails for some people and in quick glancesColor only; color + icon + labelEvery alert level has a color, an icon, and a name.
Captions need a live content sourceTranscribe audio on the phone; receive content from the venueDesigned for a venue or artist feed, like theater caption apps linked to the soundboard. On-device transcription is a backup when no feed exists.
Captions must work at any text sizeFixed sizes; Dynamic TypeThree caption sizes built on Dynamic Type, plus Reduce Motion support.

Testing and iteration

I'm testing the prototype with five Deaf and hard-of-hearing participants, measuring:

  • How many of the five haptic signals people identify correctly by feel alone
  • How quickly people notice a safety alert
  • How readable the captions are, rated 1–5

What I'm learning

Accessibility work is strongest when it treats sound as information, not just noise to caption. Designing a haptic language forced me to think about rhythm, strength, and meaning the way a composer would, and to design with Deaf and hard-of-hearing fans rather than for them.
